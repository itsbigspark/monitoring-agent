# Arc C — Decision memo

Per-slide guidance for a 6-slide decision memo delivered as slides.

The decision memo is a focused form. You're driving one specific
decision to one specific answer. Every slide either:
- Builds the context that makes the decision necessary, OR
- Frames the options the decision is choosing between, OR
- Justifies your recommendation, OR
- Names what the audience must do.

There is no other category of slide.

## Slide 1 — Cover

**Intent**: Name the decision.

Title is the decision itself, as a verb-noun question:
- "Adopt agent X as our default authoring assistant?"
- "Move the customer-data store to Region B?"
- "Pause the AML workstream until Q4?"

Subhead: the date the decision is needed by.

## Slide 2 — Context

**Intent**: Establish what the audience already accepts.

3-Column Cards. Three cards covering:
- **What we know**: the facts on the ground. Audience accepts these.
- **What we don't know**: the gaps in evidence. Audience accepts that
  these are gaps.
- **What's changed**: the new information that triggers the decision
  now. Audience accepts that something must change.

If the audience disagrees with the context, the decision is premature.
This slide *tests* the audience's alignment before you present
options.

## Slide 3 — Option set

**Intent**: Name the three options being weighed.

3-Column Cards. Three options:
- Option A
- Option B
- Option C (often "do nothing" or "delay")

Each card states the option in one verb-noun sentence + 2–3 bullets
on what it entails.

**All three options must be plausible.** Don't pad with strawmen. The
audience trusts the recommendation only if they trust the option set.

If you genuinely have only two options, fold the third card into a
"common features" panel and use two cards. Don't invent a third for
symmetry.

## Slide 4 — Recommendation

**Intent**: Advocate for one option.

Case Study layout. The recommended option:
- Title: the option name, picked from slide 3.
- Body left: why this option, in 3–4 sentences. Lead with the value
  pop, not the safety case.
- Body right: the conditions under which this option becomes wrong.
  Naming these makes the recommendation honest.

This is the most important slide. Spend half the deck-prep time on
the right-hand side — the conditions that would change the answer.

## Slide 5 — Trade-offs

**Intent**: Show what we give up.

3-Column Cards. Three cards covering the trade-offs of the
recommended option:
- **Cost** — money, time, opportunity.
- **Risk** — what we expose ourselves to.
- **Lock-in** — what becomes harder to undo.

Naming the trade-offs disarms objections. The audience reaches the
recommendation's downsides on their own before you reach slide 5
anyway; better that you've already named them.

## Slide 6 — Decision

**Intent**: Name what the audience must do, by when.

Closing layout. Three lines:
- "Decision needed: [the verb-noun]" — call it back.
- "By: [specific date]" — not "next week."
- "Owners: [names]" — who is making the call.

Optional fourth line: "Trigger if delayed: [consequence]." Useful when
the cost of delay is concrete and quantifiable.

End with the date, not with thanks.

---

## Cuts to short form (4 slides)

If the audience prefers a two-page brief:

- **Merge 2 + 3** — context + options on one slide.
- **Merge 5 + 6** — trade-offs + decision on one slide.

Result: Cover → Context-and-options → Recommendation → Trade-offs-and-decision.
