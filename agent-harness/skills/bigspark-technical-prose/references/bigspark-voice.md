# Bigspark technical voice

Use this voice for Bigspark engineering and platform documents unless a repository
or publication supplies a more specific style guide.

## Voice profile

- Lead with the outcome, decision, task, or reason the system exists.
- Write with technical confidence, but qualify uncertainty that changes a
  decision.
- Prefer plain words and precise domain terms over fashionable abstractions.
- Let evidence carry emphasis. Use adjectives only when they add testable meaning.
- Keep the reader at their expected technical altitude. Explain unfamiliar terms,
  not the terms the intended audience already uses.
- Use first person when it clarifies ownership or judgement. Do not manufacture a
  personal voice, anecdote, or opinion.
- Keep useful tension. State trade-offs and disagreement directly.

## Information rules

- Distinguish `live`, `implemented`, `specified`, `proposed`, `planned`, and
  `deprecated`.
- Name the owner, authority, or source of truth when it affects how the reader
  should act.
- Put supporting numbers, examples, links, and test evidence next to the claim.
- State boundaries explicitly: what the component owns, what it consumes, and
  what remains outside its authority.
- Separate governing rules from examples and current implementation details.
- End on a concrete conclusion, unresolved decision, or next action. Do not recap
  the document unless the audience needs a summary.

## Formatting rules

- Use sentence-case headings.
- Make headings useful navigation: state a claim, question, task, or recognisable
  document section.
- Use a table for repeated fields or exact comparisons.
- Use a diagram when relationships, sequence, or ownership are difficult to
  understand in prose.
- Use lists for genuine collections and steps. Do not convert connected reasoning
  into a list merely to make it scannable.
- Use bold sparingly. Position and structure should provide most emphasis.

## Language to challenge

Challenge these patterns in context rather than banning individual words:

- promotional significance without evidence;
- generic references to a changing "landscape";
- "not just X, but Y" and other staged contrasts;
- vague actors such as "experts", "teams", or "the industry";
- claims that a design "ensures", "guarantees", or "eliminates" a risk;
- repeated slogans or metaphors standing in for architecture;
- conclusions that repeat the introduction without adding a decision or action.

Keep a flagged phrase when it is the clearest accurate wording. Rewrite the claim,
not merely the keyword.
